SCREEN-SPACE SPLATS · VISUAL GUIDE

One ellipse.
Three ways to fade.

Every projected 3D splat gives a pixel a distance from its center. A kernel turns that distance into a weight. This project changes that weight while keeping the projected ellipse, color, and compositing rule the same.

Compare their shapes ↘
The ellipse comes from projection. The kernel decides the fade inside it.
01 / THE COMMON INPUT

Distance is measured in the ellipse’s own coordinates.

For pixel offset Δp and projected 2D covariance Σ, the renderer computes q = ΔpᵀΣ⁻¹Δp. We plot d = √q: zero at the center, one unit along a normalized ellipse axis. Every point on the same ellipse contour has the same q, so it gets the same kernel weight.

The pixel’s contribution is approximately alpha = opacity × weight(d), then front-to-back compositing mixes it with other splats. The implementation also applies an alpha cutoff and optional compact support.

02 / START HERE

Gaussian

w(d) = exp(−d² / 2)

The familiar Gaussian is a smooth bell. Squaring distance makes the weight drop faster as you move out; the exponential keeps it positive and gives it an infinitely faint tail in the mathematical function. In this renderer, a support rule and alpha threshold stop evaluating the tail after it becomes negligible.

Why it works: nearby pixels receive most of the splat’s color, while farther pixels receive smoothly less. Its rounded center and soft tail make overlap gradual.
03 / FIRST ALTERNATIVE

Linear radial

w(d) = max(0, 1 − d / √(8 ln 2))

Weight falls at a constant rate with radial distance, then reaches exactly zero at d = √(8 ln 2) ≈ 2.355. Its profile is a straight line, so the corresponding surface is a cone. The slope changes abruptly at the center and at the support boundary.

Why it works: a pixel outside the radius contributes nothing. Inside it, each extra unit of elliptical distance removes the same amount of weight.
04 / SECOND ALTERNATIVE

Quadratic (degree-2 polynomial)

w(d) = max(0, 1 − d² / R²)²

Here R² = 2 ln 2 / (1 − 1/√2) ≈ 4.733. The inner quadratic reduces the weight with squared distance; squaring the remaining value makes it approach zero smoothly at d = R ≈ 2.176. The top is rounded and the edge is flat.

Why it works: it combines a soft center with an exact finite boundary. The degree is fixed at two in this experiment; training changes splat parameters, not the degree.
05 / SHAPE LAB

Same half-height. Different shapes.

All three weights equal 0.5 at d = √(2 ln 2) ≈ 1.177. Their centers align, but their edges and total footprint differ.

Move the pixel away from the center to compare weights.

Why 0.5 there? The Gaussian reaches half height when exp(−d²/2) = 1/2, giving d² = 2 ln 2. We chose the linear support radius to be twice that distance, making its weight 1 − 1/2 there. We chose the quadratic radius so its unsquared term is 1/√2; squaring gives 1/2. This matches one width measure, not total weight or support.
